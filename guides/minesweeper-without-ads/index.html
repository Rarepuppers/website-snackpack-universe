Free Minesweeper without ads or a rigged first click

Minesweeper is one of the oldest and simplest puzzle games still played today — a grid, some numbers, no ads needed to make it interesting. That simplicity is exactly why it's been cloned thousands of times at wildly different levels of care. The most common corner cut isn't visual, it's a rule most players never think to check: whether your very first click can be a mine. Here's why that matters, and what else separates a faithful version from a rushed one.

The first-click rule most clones skip

In a properly built Minesweeper, mines aren't placed until after you click — the game waits to see where you clicked first, then lays out the board around it, guaranteeing that click is safe and usually opens up a decent starting area. It's a small piece of engineering that a lot of quick clones skip, placing all mines up front instead. The difference only shows up occasionally, but when it does, it means losing a game in the first half-second, before you've made a single real decision — which has nothing to do with skill and everything to do with a shortcut in how the board was built.

It's a good proxy for care elsewhere in the implementation, too: a site that got the first click right is more likely to have also gotten flagging, chording, and the win/loss detection right.

The ten-second check

  • Does the first click ever hit a mine? Try a few fresh boards. If you ever lose immediately, mines were placed before your click.
  • Are the classic board sizes actually classic? Beginner, Intermediate and Expert have specific, decades-old dimensions — see below.
  • Can you flag a suspected mine? Right-click on desktop or long-press on mobile should place a flag without revealing the square.
  • What happens on a loss? A full-screen ad or forced "watch to retry" prompt is a common friction point in casual puzzle clones.
  • Is there a timer and a working best-time record? A missing or broken timer suggests the rest wasn't polished either.

Two things worth knowing before you pick a site

The classic difficulty names have specific, fixed meanings. Beginner is a 9×9 grid with 10 mines. Intermediate is 16×16 with 40 mines. Expert is 30×16 with 99 mines — a wide board, not a bigger square. These sizes come from the original Windows Minesweeper and have stayed the standard for decades. Some free sites reuse the labels "Beginner/Intermediate/Expert" on different grid sizes or mine densities, which quietly changes the difficulty curve and makes your times incomparable to anyone playing the real thing.

Minesweeper is mostly skill, with one honest exception. Once you're a few clicks in, the numbers usually give you enough information to work out mine positions with certainty — that's the whole game. Genuine unavoidable 50/50 guesses do occasionally happen deep in the endgame, and that's a legitimate part of the puzzle, not a flaw. The first-click guarantee exists specifically to remove the one piece of pure luck that isn't supposed to be there: losing before you've had any information to reason from at all.

Common questions

Can you lose Minesweeper on your very first click?

Not in a well-built version — mines are placed after your first click so it's always safe. Some free clones place mines up front, which means the opening move can be pure luck.

What are the real classic board sizes?

Beginner is 9×9 with 10 mines, Intermediate is 16×16 with 40 mines, Expert is 30×16 with 99 mines — the same dimensions as the original Windows Minesweeper.

Is Minesweeper luck or skill?

Mostly skill — the numbers usually let you deduce mine positions with certainty. Occasional genuine 50/50 guesses in the endgame are normal; losing on an unrevealed first click isn't.

What does flagging do?

Marks a square you believe is a mine so you don't click it by accident. It doesn't change the board, just helps you track your own deductions.
