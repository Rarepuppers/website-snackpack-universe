What order are phonics sounds taught in?

Phonics does not start at A. It starts with a small set of sounds chosen so that a child can read real words almost immediately. Here is the usual opening sequence, what each set unlocks, and why programmes differ.

Why not A, B, C?

Alphabetical order is an efficient way to look words up and a poor way to learn to read. It front-loads letters that build almost nothing: a child who knows only a, b and c cannot write a single common word.

Systematic phonics programmes therefore introduce sounds in an order chosen so that real words become readable as early as possible, usually within the first few lessons.

Why s a t p i n comes first

This set is the most widely used opening group, and the reasons are practical rather than arbitrary:

  • They combine into many words — sat, pin, tap, nip, pit, tin, spit and more.
  • They include two vowels, a and i, so words can be built immediately.
  • Several can be held — s, n and a can be stretched aloud, which makes blending easier to hear than with abrupt sounds.
  • They are visually distinct, avoiding easily confused shapes early on.

Where the five short vowels appear

Across the three sets above, every short vowel is covered:

  • a and i arrive together in set 1 — "Pip Sits" leans on short i, "Nan and Pip" on short a.
  • e arrives in set 2, carried by "Ten Red Hens".
  • o and u complete the set in set 3, in "Dog in the Fog" and "Bug in a Cup".

A child who can read all five short vowels in simple three-sound words has the foundation that later patterns are built on.

Programmes differ, and that is fine

There is no single official order. Widely used programmes open with the same broad logic but diverge in the detail, and a school following one scheme will sequence sounds differently from a school following another.

So a "stage 2" book from one publisher will not line up with stage 2 from another. Match books to the sounds your child has actually been taught, not to a stage number — and if your child is at school, their sequence is the one worth following at home.

Tricky words sit outside the sequence

Some very common words cannot be sounded out with the sounds taught so far — the, was, said, one. Programmes usually introduce a small number of these alongside each set, to be learned by sight for now so that sentences sound natural.

These are a deliberate small exception to the sequence, not evidence that the sequence has failed.
