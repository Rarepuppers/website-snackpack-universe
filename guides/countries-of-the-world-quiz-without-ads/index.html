A countries of the world quiz without ads or a sign-in

Play two different countries-of-the-world quizzes and you can get two different totals: 193, 195, 196, 197. None of them is a typo. The number of countries is a decision about which list you are using, and a quiz that never tells you which one it picked is asking you to memorise its private opinion. Here is what actually varies, and what a fair version does about it.

Why the count changes from quiz to quiz

The one number with a precise definition is 193: the member states of the United Nations. Add the two non-member observer states — the Holy See and the State of Palestine — and you get 195, which is where most published lists stop.

Beyond that it is genuinely contested. Taiwan is governed independently but is not a UN member. Kosovo is recognised by many states and not by others. Western Sahara's status is unresolved. Depending on which of these a quiz includes, its total lands anywhere from 193 to about 197 — and every one of those totals can be defended, which is exactly why a quiz should say which it chose rather than marking you wrong from an unstated list.

This matters more than it sounds. If a quiz counts 196 and you learned 195, you will lose points on a question about a place whose status is a live diplomatic dispute, not a fact you failed to learn.

The ten-second check

  • Does it name its list? “UN members” or “UN members plus observers” is a real answer. A bare total with no source is not.
  • Does it accept alternate names? Czechia and the Czech Republic are the same country; so are Myanmar and Burma, and eSwatini and Swaziland. Rejecting the other name tests spelling, not geography.
  • Which projection is the map drawn in? A Mercator world map makes high-latitude countries look enormous. If the quiz never says, you are learning its distortion along with its geography.
  • Does a miss teach you anything? A wrong answer that just goes red is a scoring event. A wrong answer that shows you where the country actually is, is a lesson.
  • Does it need an account before you can play? A geography quiz has no technical reason to require a sign-in.

Two things worth knowing before you start

The count is a choice, not a fact. There is no world authority that issues the official number of countries. There is a UN membership list, there are recognition decisions made state by state, and there are cartographic conventions. When someone says “there are 195 countries” they are quoting UN members plus the two observer states, which is a reasonable convention and still a convention.

Recognising a shape is not the same as knowing where it is. Most quizzes test the first and let you believe you have learned the second. The classic demonstration is size: on the Mercator projection almost every world map uses, Greenland looks roughly the size of Africa. Africa is about fourteen times larger. If your mental map came from a Mercator quiz, that distortion came with it — which is a good reason to practise on a globe as well as a flat map.

Common questions

How many countries are there in the world?

193 states are members of the United Nations. Adding the two non-member observer states, the Holy See and the State of Palestine, gives 195, which is the most commonly published figure. Lists that also count Taiwan, Kosovo or Western Sahara reach 196 or 197. There is no single official answer, so a quiz should tell you which list it is scoring against.

Why do different geography quizzes give different totals?

Because they start from different lists. The difference is almost never a mistake about geography; it is a difference about recognition and sovereignty, which is a political question rather than a cartographic one.

Does the map projection change what I learn?

Yes. The Mercator projection used by most flat world maps exaggerates area near the poles, which is why Greenland can look comparable in size to Africa when Africa is roughly fourteen times larger. Practising on a globe as well as a flat map corrects for it.
