Water Sort strategy: solve the board without extra tubes

Water Sort looks like a game of obvious matching moves until both empty tubes are occupied and the colour you need is trapped three layers down. The difference between a clean solve and a restart is usually not a clever final move. It is how you treated the spare space ten pours earlier.

The complete rule

You may pour only the connected run at the top of a tube. The destination must be empty or show that same colour on top, and it must have room. If three blue layers are connected but the destination has space for only two, two move and one remains. You cannot reach a buried colour until everything above it has moved legally.

That means each empty tube is more than storage: it is temporary access to whatever sits underneath a top layer. Good play asks not only “where can this colour go?” but “what becomes available after it moves, and can I free this buffer again?”

The three-question check

  • Does this join a colour? Combining matching runs reduces fragmentation.
  • What does it reveal? A newly exposed colour should have a useful destination.
  • How does the buffer empty? Before placing a colour in the last empty tube, know the move that releases it.

1. Keep one tube flexible

On a board with two empty tubes, it is tempting to treat both as free parking. That often creates the first real dead end. If you put red in one and green in the other while their matching layers remain buried, every other tube must now accept only its own top colour. A single mismatch can stop all progress.

Use an empty tube with an exit plan. Suppose moving orange exposes blue, and blue can immediately pour onto another blue run. That sequence is productive: the orange temporarily occupies the buffer, the blue consolidation creates space, and the orange can later move onto its match. A random top colour with no visible partner is much more expensive.

2. Consolidate before you complete

Joining two matching top runs is usually strong because it creates room and turns scattered pieces into one block. But filling a tube to four is not automatically the best move. If completing purple consumes the only open destination while the remaining tubes have incompatible tops, you may have finished one colour and frozen five others. Look one move beyond the satisfying completion.

Once a tube is complete, leave it alone unless breaking it clearly unlocks the board. A finished tube reduces the active puzzle by one colour and one container. Repeatedly borrowing from completed colours makes the position harder to reason about.

3. Work from exposed information

You do not need to memorise every hidden layer. Focus on the top two. A useful move either joins the visible top colour or reveals a second colour that has somewhere to go. If neither is true, the pour probably only rearranges the problem.

Harder boards sometimes require a temporary “wrong-looking” move into an empty tube. That is fine when it opens a chain: reveal blue, join blue, expose yellow, finish yellow, then return the temporary colour. The empty tube is a workspace, not a trophy to keep untouched forever.

Common Water Sort dead ends

  • Two blocked buffers: both empty tubes hold colours with no accessible match.
  • Premature completion: a finished tube used the only space needed to untangle another colour.
  • Moving a full run to an empty tube: the board changed position but gained no new access or space.
  • Chasing one colour: repeatedly uncovering blue while ignoring that each displaced layer also needs a legal home.
  • Restarting instead of undoing: the last useful fork is often only two or three pours back.

When you are stuck

  1. Scan for matching colours already on top.
  2. Find a short run whose removal reveals one of those matches.
  3. Check whether an empty tube can hold it temporarily.
  4. If every option consumes the final buffer without creating space, undo to before that buffer was filled.
  5. Reset only when you want a clean attempt; a failed line is useful information.

Common questions

What is the best first move?

Prefer a pour that joins matching top colours or exposes another useful match. Avoid occupying both empty tubes with colours that have no accessible partner.

Can every puzzle be solved without an extra tube?

Only if its construction guarantees that. SnackPack boards are scrambled from solved states using reversible moves, so their original tube count is sufficient.

Should I fill an empty tube immediately?

Usually not without an exit plan. Use the tube to expose buried colours, but know where its temporary contents can move next.
