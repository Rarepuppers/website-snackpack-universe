How chess puzzles work

Each puzzle is a position where one side can force checkmate. The banner tells you who is to move and how many moves you have. In a mate in one, a single move ends the game. In a mate in two or mate in three, you play a key move, the computer answers with its most stubborn defence, and you carry on until it is mate. Only a first move that wins against every defence counts, so a check that lets the king escape is marked as not quite right.

Tap one of your pieces to see where it can go, then tap a highlighted square. Hint lights up the piece that starts the winning idea. After each solve you get a short explanation of why it works, which is how these patterns stick.

The puzzles

  • Daily: one mate in one and one mate in two, new every day and the same for everyone. Each has exactly one winning first move.
  • Starter: 17 back-rank and edge mates to warm up, with an explanation for each.
  • Opening traps: 12 famous early blunders that end in checkmate, like Fool's Mate and Scholar's Mate.
  • Mate in two: 15 two-move finishes with a key move, a reply and mate.
  • Mate in three: 6 longer combinations for when two moves feel easy.

Patterns worth knowing

  • Back-rank mate. A king behind its own unmoved pawns has no escape square, so a rook or queen on the back rank mates.
  • King and queen. Take squares away first, then bring your king close enough to protect the queen's final check.
  • The rook ladder. Two rooks drive a king to the edge: one guards a rank while the other checks on the next.
  • Quiet moves. In many mate in two puzzles the key move is not a check at all. It takes away the last flight square, and mate follows whatever Black does.

Why was my move marked wrong when it gave check?

In a mate in two or three, the first move has to force mate against every reply. A check that lets the king slip away is not enough, even if it looks strong.
